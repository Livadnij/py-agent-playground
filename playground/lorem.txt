Sourdough bread depends on patience more than on skill. The dough is made from only flour, water and salt, and it rises because of a living culture of wild yeast and bacteria that you keep alive by feeding it regularly. After mixing, the dough rests for several hours while the culture slowly builds flavor and structure. Folding it gently every half hour strengthens the gluten without the need for hard kneading. Many bakers then let the shaped loaf sit in the refrigerator overnight, which deepens the taste and makes the dough easier to score. In the morning the loaf goes into a very hot oven, ideally inside a heavy covered pot that traps steam and gives the crust its crackle. The first few loaves are often dense or pale, but each one teaches you how your kitchen, your flour and your starter behave. After a handful of bakes, the rhythm becomes familiar, and the smell of a fresh loaf cooling on the counter feels like a fair reward for the wait.

Regular maintenance is what keeps a small software project healthy as it grows. When a codebase is young, every file is easy to hold in your head, and shortcuts feel harmless. Over time those shortcuts pile up, and a change in one place starts to break something far away. The habit that prevents this is simple. Keep each module responsible for one thing, let dependencies point in a single direction, and name things so that the purpose is obvious without a comment. Write down the decisions that are not obvious, because the reason behind a choice is the first thing to disappear from memory. Review the project every few weeks and remove whatever no longer earns its place. A smaller codebase with clear boundaries is faster to change, easier to test, and far less stressful to return to after a break.
